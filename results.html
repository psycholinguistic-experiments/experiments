                      These analyses were motivated by patterns noticed after inspecting the class results and are exploratory. They should be treated as hypotheses for future work rather than confirmatory evidence.

                      Show the exploratory analyses

                      1 · Do English chains converge on a smaller response vocabulary?

                      For each student and language: how often a response recurs across the student’s own different chains (cross-chain reuse), the share of distinct responses, and how often an answer repeats its chain’s starting word or an earlier answer in the same chain (within-chain repetition). This is reuse within one person’s chains, not a vocabulary shared across students.

                        Definitions and the full table

                        2 · Does lexical convergence go with the starting-word decay difference?

                        The starting-word model again, with every term also multiplied by each student’s English − Chinese difference in cross-chain reuse (centred), and a by-student random slope for the decay term. The question: do students whose English chains reuse more across chains (relative to their Chinese chains) also show a larger English − Chinese difference in how fast the starting-word gap shrinks?

                          3 · Are L1 (Chinese) dynamics associated with bilingual experience?

                          Chinese chains only, with three measures of bilingual experience: English age of acquisition, current English use, and the English − Chinese overall self-rating. Each is tested separately against three features of the Chinese chains, a family of nine tests judged on Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values. These data cannot distinguish bilingualism-induced L1 change from pre-existing individual differences because there is no monolingual comparison group.

                            4 · Semantic-space analysis (not done)

                            Exact-response reuse is not semantic convergence. Testing whether English chains move away from the starting concept faster, or explore less of the semantic space, needs a validated multilingual embedding model that places Chinese and English words and short phrases in one space, applied with the same preprocessing to both languages and run offline into a fixed table of every response (updated when a class adds new responses). The page has no such model, and running one in the browser would make it slow, so this is left for future work.

                              Data quality

                              Answers scored: found in the valence norms (Warriner et al., 2013; Chan & Tse, 2024). Valid transitions: pairs of adjacent answers that were both scored, out of 90 per student and language (answers 1→2 to 9→10 in ten chains). An unscored answer removes the transitions on either side of it; transitions are never bridged across a missing valence rating.

                              Methods and full statistical output

                              Methods and model specifications

                              Scoring

                              Each answer is looked up in the valence norms: Warriner et al. (2013) for English (after lower-casing, British → American spelling and lemmatising), Chan & Tse (2024) for Chinese (simplified or traditional form). Answers the norms do not rate stay in their chain as missing.

                              The Chinese norms are more compressed around the middle of the scale, so they are linearly mapped onto the English normative scale before cross-language comparison. The map gives Chinese ratings the English mean and standard deviation over the two whole norm sets: English-scale valence = −1.53 + 1.31 × Chinese valence (assets/data/equating.json). The percentile-equating check in Robustness uses a non-linear map instead.

                              States: negative below 5, positive 5 and above (on the English scale). Transitions are pairs of adjacent scored answers, from answer 1→2 to 9→10; the starting word → answer 1 step is not counted.

                              Coding

                              Language: English = +½, Chinese = −½. Previous state: positive = +½, negative = −½. Block: second language done = +½, first = −½. Seed valence: the starting word’s valence on the English scale, minus its mean over the rows the model uses. Every model has random intercepts for students, starting words (seeds) and chains (one student’s ten answers to one starting word).

                              Design limitation: the starting words

                              Chinese and English used separate matched sets of starting words rather than translation equivalents. Language comparisons therefore generalise across these matched sets, but cannot completely separate language from item-set differences. The random intercept for starting words absorbs variation among the words; it does not turn the comparison into an item-matched one.

                              Transition model (logistic)

                              logit P(next answer positive) = β0 + β1·Language + β2·Previous state + β3·Language × Previous state + β4·Seed valence + β5·Position + β6·Block + ustudent + useed + uchain

                              One row per valid transition. Position: the earlier answer’s position minus 5 (transitions 1→2 to 9→10). Laplace approximation, with β estimated jointly with the random effects (as lme4’s nAGQ = 0).

                              Continuous-valence check (linear)

                              Next valence = β0 + β1·Language + β2·(Previous valence − 5) + β3·Language × (Previous valence − 5) + β4·Seed valence + β5·Position + β6·Block + ustudent + useed + uchain + ε

                              Trajectory model (linear)

                              Valence = β0 + β1·Language + β2·P + β3·(P² − 8.25) + β4·Language × P + β5·Language × (P² − 8.25) + β6·Seed valence + β7·Block + ustudent + useed + uchain + ε

                              P = position − 5.5. One row per scored answer (positions 1–10). Because P is centred at the middle of the chain and the model also has a curve, β4 is the language difference in slope at the centre of the chain, not a constant difference from position 1 to 10. The sensitivity check replaces P and P² with position as a category (answer 1 the reference).

                              Starting-word model (linear)

                              Valence = β0 + β1·Language + β2·Seed valence + β3·log2(position) + β4·Language × Seed valence + β5·Language × log2(position) + β6·Seed valence × log2(position) + β7·Language × Seed valence × log2(position) + β8·Block + ustudent + useed + uchain + ε

                              log2(position) is 0 at answer 1, so β4 is the language difference in the starting word’s influence on the first answer, and β7 the language difference in how that influence changes each time the position doubles. The separation plot multiplies the model’s influence per point of seed valence by the gap between the mean valence of all positive and all negative starting words (both languages’ words together, the same value for both languages).

                              Proficiency models (exploratory)

                              logit P(next answer positive) = [the transition model’s terms] + β7·R + β8·Language × R + β9·Previous state × R + β10·Language × Previous state × R + ustudent + ustudent,L·Language + ustudent,LP·(Language × Previous state) + useed + uchain

                              Valence = [the trajectory model’s terms] + β8·R + β9·Language × R + ustudent + ustudent,L·Language + useed + uchain + ε

                              R = the student’s English self-rating (0–10) minus the mean over the students in the model; one pair of models per rating. The rating varies only between students, so the language terms get by-student random slopes (uncorrelated, as lme4’s ||); without them the standard errors of these cross-level interactions would be too small. Change in the English − Chinese difference per rating point: staying positive β8 + ½β10, staying negative −β8 + ½β10 (log-odds, Wald z); mean valence β9 (valence points, Satterthwaite t). The plots’ lines are the models’ English − Chinese differences at each rating (average marginal predictions for the transition model). Every student’s transitions count, weighted by how many they have, rather than one proportion per student. The overall rating is primary (three tests, unadjusted); the four skills’ twelve tests are judged on Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values. This is an exploratory moderation analysis: ratings are self-reports, and the class was not sampled to vary in proficiency.

                              Response timing

                              log(median time) = β0 + β1·Language + β2·Block + β3·Language × Block + ustudent + ε

                              Fitted separately for onset (answer shown → first keystroke) and total time (answer shown → answer submitted), on each student’s median per language: two rows per student. Block: second language done = +½, first = −½, from the order the student did the languages in. With two rows per student, the language × block term is a between-student comparison: the two order groups differ by β3/2 in mean log time, shown as the ratio exp(β3/2), Chinese-first ÷ English-first. The block term is the practice effect averaged over the two languages; language-specific practice effects are not separable from the order groups here. Predicted times are exp(β0 ± β1/2), geometric means over students averaged over blocks; their difference in seconds has a delta-method CI; the other terms are shown as ratios, exp(β). Only these medians are stored with each student’s summary. Every answer’s onset and submit times are also kept in the data sheet (its fast_trials tab), but this page cannot read that tab, so no item-level timing model is fitted. The difference between a student’s median total time and median onset is not a median completion time and is not reported as one.

                              Lexical reuse (exploratory)

                              Three measures per student and language (defined under “What might produce the trajectory difference?”), each compared English − Chinese with a paired t-test, for all students and without the students with extreme repetition; Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the three measures within each sample. Sensitivity: raw, normalised and lemma response keys.

                              Exploratory models: reuse × starting-word decay, and L1 dynamics

                              Valence = [the starting-word model’s terms] + M × [each of them] + ustudent + ustudent,LSP·(Language × Seed valence × log2(position)) + useed + uchain + ε

                              M = the student’s English − Chinese cross-chain reuse (percentage points, normalised responses), centred. Key term: Language × Seed valence × log2(position) × M, reported in points of the gap’s change (answer 1 → 10) per percentage point of M.

                              Chinese only: Valence = β0 + β1·Seed valence + β2·log2(position) + β3·Seed valence × log2(position) + β4·Block + X × [β5 + β6·Seed valence + β7·log2(position) + β8·Seed valence × log2(position)] + ustudent + ustudent,SP·(Seed valence × log2(position)) + useed + uchain + ε

                              Chinese only: Valence = β0 + β1·P + β2·(P² − 8.25) + β3·Seed valence + β4·Block + X × [β5 + β6·P + β7·(P² − 8.25)] + ustudent + ustudent,P·P + useed + uchain + ε

                              X: English age of acquisition (years), English use (per 10 percentage points), or English − Chinese overall self-rating, each centred and tested in its own models. Key terms: β8 (starting-word persistence) and the joint test of β6 and β7 (trajectory shape). The third outcome, Chinese cross-chain reuse, is regressed on X by least squares with HC3 errors (one value per student). Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across the nine tests. In every model with a student-level moderator, the term it moderates gets a by-student random slope (uncorrelated), so that the interaction’s standard error reflects differences between students.

                              Exclusion criteria

                              Applied before every analysis on the page (the CSV downloads keep every student, with columns analysed and excluded_for). A student is excluded when (1) fewer than 70% of their possible transitions (adjacent answers both scored for valence) are usable in either language; (2) more than half of their answers in a language repeat the chain’s starting word or an earlier answer in the same chain (the site’s repetition measure); or (3) their first-language answer here contradicts the same code’s answer to the Judgement task (“Is Chinese your first language?”), where both are loaded on the page. Response times are not trimmed or capped. The robustness column “All students (no exclusions)” refits the main models on everyone. By-student random slopes (robustness): uncorrelated slopes for the language terms named in the robustness notes, added to each primary model.

                              Estimates, tests and intervals

                              Linear models: REML; t-tests and 95% CIs use Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (as lmerTest). Logistic model: Wald z and 95% CIs.

                              Model-adjusted probabilities (logistic model): average marginal predictions. Every observed transition is predicted with language and previous state set as stated, random effects at zero, keeping its own seed valence, position and block; the predictions are averaged. CIs by the delta method. Model-adjusted valence (linear models): the same averaging, which for a linear model equals the prediction at the observed means of the other predictors.

                              Omnibus tests are joint Wald tests that several coefficients are all zero. Transition pattern: Language and Language × Previous state (2 df, χ²). Trajectory shape: Language × P and Language × (P² − 8.25) (an F-test with Satterthwaite denominator degrees of freedom, as lmerTest’s contest). “Clear evidence” means a joint p < .05 for an omnibus test, and a 95% CI excluding zero for a single estimate or contrast. The page’s sentences follow the same rule: a sentence says two things differ, or that something rises, falls or shrinks, only when that difference or change has clear evidence; otherwise it gives the estimate and says it is not clear. No size word (“small”, “similar”, “much”) comes from a cut-off. Display-only rules, which change no conclusion: the note on observed vs model-adjusted probabilities appears when they differ at two decimals; students who repeated an earlier word in more than half of their answers are flagged under data quality; classes under 10 students get a “small class” note; CI bounds that round to zero get a third decimal. Every model and joint test was checked against lme4 and lmerTest in R on the class data, to the reported precision.
